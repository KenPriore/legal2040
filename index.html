Money Matters

A story from 2040 about what happened to American law

In 2040, nearly every commercial dispute in America is resolved by machine, in minutes, for pennies, with a reasoned and unsigned opinion. The matters that turn on a human life, guilt, liberty, ruin, mercy, are still heard by people, slowly, in rooms. A reporter follows a retired chief legal officer through the rise of the money machines and into the blurred country where money, humanity, and justice refuse to come apart, and asks who decides which line you stand in.

On a Tuesday in October, in two rooms 1,100 miles apart, there were two disputes that were decided. One took four minutes, and the other took most of the day.

In the first room, which is not a room at all, but a phone in the hand of a florist named Rosa Delgado, in a strip mall outside Phoenix. The dispute was a four-year supply contract that went sideways. Her wholesaler had changed a delivery term and billed her for the difference. Delgado tapped a button. The system pulled the contract, which had been enforcing its own terms the entire time; read the messages between the parties; applied the governing standard; and rendered a decision, with reasons, that she could read, understand, and appeal if she wished. The reasons ran to a page. The part she showed me said: “The delivery term was changed on March 3 by the seller without the buyer’s written agreement. Under section 4 of the contract, a change to a delivery term requires written agreement. The charge for the difference is not owed. The buyer may appeal this decision.” The argument was over in four minutes. It cost her nothing. “My father lost a restaurant to a lawsuit he won. He won,” she told me, stripping thorns while she talked, because the interview was costing her an afternoon and the roses did not care. “Being right cost more than being wrong would have. I still can’t believe that’s not the world anymore.”

In the second room, an actual room, with oak panels and a flag and a dozen people on the benches, a judge took most of a day over an eviction. The arrears came to four thousand one hundred and eighty dollars, and nobody disputed the figure. The question was whether the amount should be allowed to put a family on the street in November. No machine was allowed near this action.

Every dispute in America now goes through one of two windows. The first free window takes four minutes to render an opinion. The second window has fees, attorneys, a wait, and people behind it. And society is okay with that, for now.

The usual account of what has happened to American law in the last fifteen years is that the country split it in two: it handed money to the machines, contracts, commerce, the vast arithmetic of who-owes-whom, and kept the questions of guilt and liberty and justice for human beings in rooms. Both rooms were about money. One was only money, and a machine closed it in four minutes. The other had a family inside the number, and so it went to a human being and took a day. The sorting does not run cleanly between money and justice. It runs through the middle of money itself: at some point a sum stops being a sum and becomes a life, and a decision has to be made about which window it goes to. No statute says where that point is.

I spent the better part of a year reporting on which side of that line things fell, and why, and I did most of it in the company of a woman who signed the order closing her company’s transactional group in 2033. Her name is Margaret Halloran, and for the back half of her career she was the chief legal officer of one of the largest corporations on earth.

I.The Seat

“You have to understand the seat I was sitting in,” Halloran told me, the first time we met, at her house outside Cincinnati. She served coffee in mugs from a law firm that no longer exists. Neither of us commented on them. She is tall, deliberate, with the courtroom habit of pausing exactly one beat longer than feels comfortable. “I didn’t make laws. I was general counsel at a corporation. I made peace between the company and the world, between what the business wants to do and what it’s allowed to do, and often just peace between the people inside the company. For two hundred years that peace was made out of paper and people, and my whole job was paper and people, and so quickly it dissolved.”

The story she tells starts, ironically for someone who talks about paper and people, with an email. It was from the firm of Ashland & Pike, announcing that it would spend half a billion dollars building proprietary technology, and Halloran forwarded it to her team with a joke. Even then she was seen as something of a laggard for still using email. Everyone else had moved on to the messaging systems, and a lot of people were already talking to their computers instead of writing to anyone. Maybe, she says now, she should have paid more attention to that. She found the email eventually, in an archive she is not supposed to still have access to. The subject line was “half a billion dollars for autocorrect.” Fourteen people replied. Two of them now work for the company that built the system her old department runs on.

But the seat she sat in gave her a particular angle on the change, because she watched it arrive in order. First the machines drafted. Then they connected the documents to one another, the contract to the amendment to the message that had changed a term. Then they reasoned, and the memos coming back were better than the ones her lawyers were writing, and her lawyers were doing less of the work and more of the reading. “There was a year when the best argument in the building was coming out of the system and a lawyer was presenting it,” she said. “Then there was a year when I noticed the system’s judgment was better than ours, and it was cheaper. Once both of those were true, there wasn’t a conversation left to have. A contract is a promise with a number attached, and the thing about a number is that a machine can hold it accurately, forever, without getting bored or getting greedy or going home at five. I think that’s the whole story. We automated the part of the law that was already math. It just took us a while to admit how much of it was.”

The order matters, because each step fed the next. The machines learned to read agreements and to pull the numbers out of them correctly, then to connect what one system knew to what another did, the contract to the invoice to the shipping record, and to put the result in front of a lawyer in better shape than any associate had ever managed. The lawyer decided. The machine watched the decision. After enough decisions it began to recommend one, and the recommendations were right often enough, and fast enough, that people stopped checking them. Their judgment had become another set of data.

There is a detail Halloran returns to whenever I press her on the true origin of all this, and it is dull. Data portability. The procurement-desk right to take your own information and walk out the door with it. “Nothing happens without it,” she said. “None of it. You can build the smartest system on earth, and if it can’t get at the data, clean, portable, yours, it’s a very expensive paperweight. The whole revolution rode in on a contract clause that put people to sleep in 2024.” I heard a version of that sentence from Stein, from Venkatesan, and from a regulator in Brussels.

Portability only got the data moving. “Everybody thinks the trick was getting the machine to read the documents,” Halloran said. “Reading was never the hard part. The hard part was the relationships, taking a pile of fragmented, inconsistent, half-scanned public records and turning them into something true. A clean family tree. A timeline that actually holds. A claim chart that survives a fight. The exact shape the work needs, not a summary of it.” For years, she said, the models could find almost anything and structure almost nothing, they would happily summarize a record and just as happily get the relationships wrong. The turn came when they got good enough to do the structuring: to convert the mess into reliable infrastructure. “The day the machine stopped describing the data and started organizing it correctly,” she said, “was the day my profession ended and didn’t notice.”

II.The Nudge

The commercial side began in 2027 at the National Arbitration Association, an institution most people had never heard of, which that year began automating the resolution of small commercial disputes: the kind that pile up by the millions in online commerce, where a consumer and a seller disagree about a charge, a return, a term of service, and where the dollar amounts are too small to justify a lawyer and too numerous to ignore. The N.A.A. built systems that could read the transaction record, apply the governing rules, and issue a binding award, at the scale and speed that e-commerce actually moved.

“Everybody fixated on the courts later,” said Hannah Stein, who ran the initiative and now sits on three boards. We met in a hotel bar in Philadelphia between two of them. “But the courts were never going to go first. They’re too slow and too proud. We went first because we had to. We had four hundred arbitrators. There were a hundred million disputes a year. Do the math. They were just evaporating. People eating losses because justice cost more than the loss. We didn’t set out to remake the law. We set out to clear a backlog the size of the ocean.”

What Stein’s team proved, for a whole class of disputes, the money kind, the kind where the question is simply who owes what, was that an automated process was better: faster, cheaper, more consistent, and, because it could explain itself, often fairer than the human version it replaced, which had run on exhaustion, caseload, and whoever could afford the better lawyer. “We expected people to hate it,” Stein said. “Some did. But most people, when their forty-dollar dispute got resolved in an afternoon with an explanation they could read, did not write us angry letters about the sanctity of the common law. They wrote thank-you notes. Actual thank-you notes. I kept a folder.” She smiled. “Someone on my team charted it, complaints against thank-yous, one line crossing the other. The week they crossed, I stopped taking calls from the bar associations.” She kept the reversals too. In the first eighteen months the N.A.A. vacated eleven hundred of its own awards, most of them because the system had read a shipping timestamp in the wrong time zone. “We fixed it in a weekend,” she said. “A weekend. Eleven hundred people got a letter.”

Hundreds of millions of people had spent two decades settling small grievances inside platforms, refunds, disputes, chargebacks, long before they would have called it arbitration. The N.A.A. added notice, a record, and an appeal window. By 2031 a generation of consumers had been trained to accept that a money disagreement could be resolved by a system, in private, in minutes.

Why arbitration went first

The courts were too slow and too proud to lead. Arbitration led because it had a backlog it could not clear any other way, and because a generation had already been trained by refunds and chargebacks to accept a system settling a money disagreement in minutes.

III.The Tipping

In the spring of 2032, in a conference room in Cincinnati, Margaret Halloran’s finance team put a spreadsheet in front of her showing that the law firms she had spent thirty years cultivating were a breach of her duty to the company. By then the proprietary platforms could do the whole commercial job, draft, negotiate, redline, close, better and faster than the firms could, and cheaper by an amount the spreadsheet had worked out to the dollar. “And the spreadsheet was right,” she said. “That’s the part nobody warns you about. The machine doesn’t beat you in an argument. It just makes the argument disappear.”

➤See Condition 6 · The economics have to flip.

Her company became one of the first of its size to retain no outside human counsel for transactional matters, to route the entire commercial life of a global corporation through a system. The clause that made it possible was one she had insisted on in the 2029 renewals, as leverage: data portability, the right to take the company’s own legal history and feed it to whatever engine she chose. “I built the road they drove over me with,” she said, without bitterness. “And I’d do it again. It was the right call. It’s just a strange thing to be proud of.”

The transactional group closed on a Thursday in March of 2033, and she remembers it by the catering. “We had the good sandwiches,” she said. “Somebody in facilities had a budget code that still worked.” There were sixty-two lawyers on the floor that morning and a memo that had gone out at six, which she had written, and rewritten, and then let the system rewrite, because its version was kinder and she could not stand that it was kinder. She showed me both versions on a tablet. Hers used the word transition four times. The system’s used it once, and opened with a sentence hers did not have: “This decision is not a judgment about your work.” She said she still cannot tell whether that sentence was true. She read her own aloud anyway. “I got through the first paragraph. Then a woman named Teresa, contracts, twenty-two years, stood up and said, ‘Margaret, we’ve all read it.’ That was the speech.” Forty-four of the sixty-two were placed inside the company within the year, as contract operations analysts and systems reviewers. Nine went to firms that were themselves gone by 2037. She keeps the list. “People think the decision was the hard part. The decision was a spreadsheet. The room was the hard part.” She was quiet for a moment. “I miss the binders. The closing binders. One of the firms did them in blue leather with the deal name in gold, and I put them on a shelf, and I have not missed one thing about that job except the shelf.”

The displacement had faces. Marcus Bell, then a commercial litigator, was one of two hundred reassigned in a single memo from “litigation” to “strategy.” “They didn’t fire us, which would at least have been clear,” he said. “They told us we were being elevated. I had a trial record and they wanted me on sales calls.” It took him a year to stop being furious and another to admit that the machines had taken the part of the job he’d thought was the job, the drafting, the filing, the arguing, and left him the part he hadn’t known was there. “The part where you decide what’s even worth fighting about,” he said. “Turns out that part was always the job. The rest was just expensive typing.”

The Patent Office automated its examinations in 2029, out of desperation. Filings had spiked past anything the human examiner corps could absorb, and patent data is the canonical mess, a century of continuations and counterparts and scanned PDFs that only make sense when you connect them across systems never built to talk to one another. The agents could hold all of it, and they could structure it, and once the structuring was built into the review itself the backlog simply ceased to exist, and approvals started arriving with reasons a person could follow.

In Maryland, a state forever overshadowed by Delaware’s corporate franchise, a judge named Antoine Reyes built a system, clumsily branded Mary-Tech, that let parties run a commercial dispute through an agentic process end to end, transparent and appealable. He is aware the name is terrible. “My clerk had a list of better ones. I don’t remember them now. I wanted people to underestimate the thing for as long as possible.” Reyes is emphatic about the word commercial. “I never touched the criminal docket,” he told me. “I never wanted to. I built a machine to decide who owed whom for a shipment of steel. That is a question with an answer. ‘Did this man intend to kill that one.’ That is not,” and he stopped for a moment. “It is not that kind of question. Anyone who tells you it is has never sat through a sentencing.” Within two years, so many corporations had relocated their commercial disputes to Maryland that in 2035 Delaware amended its Chancery rules to match.

The fiduciary fact

The economics stopped being a preference and became a duty. Keeping the firms she had spent thirty years cultivating was, on the spreadsheet, a breach of her duty to the company.

The clause that did it

The portability right she had negotiated as leverage became the fuel that made her own department obsolete. She would do it again.

IV.The Correction

Lorraine Pasko ran payables for a mid-sized importer in Savannah, and what she remembers of the Correction is a screen. On the second Tuesday of the crisis she watched a routine supplier contract, ninety days, steel fittings, reprice itself four times before lunch. “The contract was doing what it was built to do,” she said. “It had a clause for currency moves. Nobody had ever seen the clause run. It ran, and it ran, and by the fourth time the number didn’t mean anything to me anymore, it was just a number changing, and I remember thinking, well, at least somebody’s sure.” She did what payables had always done with a contract behaving strangely: at twenty to ten she sent it to outside counsel with three questions, whether they could hold payment, whether they could get out of the contract, and whether the clause was even enforceable. The answer came back the next morning, and it was a good answer, and by then it was about nothing. In the day between, the dollar had fallen further than it had in the whole of her working life, and the kill switches and circuit breakers written into contracts across the country, in case of a day nobody planned for, had all tripped at once. The third reprice had already been disputed, by the contract itself, with the supplier’s system, and settled at the first window in four minutes, and paid. “The memo said we could contest the third one,” she said. “We had. The contract had. It was done before the lawyers finished reading it.” That week her company stopped sending anything to outside counsel, not as a decision but as a reflex. “We never went back. Nobody said we weren’t going back. We just, didn’t.”

The Correction was the currency seizure of 2036, when the dollar stopped holding its value from one hour to the next and the country rebuilt the financial machinery it had assumed was eternal. Its effect on the law was that the money side finished automating in about eighteen months.

The Internal Revenue Service was abolished in its old form and reborn as a system that rewrote its own code each year, learning, closing its own loopholes, growing measurably fairer per cycle. Priya Venkatesan led the rebuild. She dislikes being called the woman who turned off the I.R.S., and when I used the phrase she corrected me. “Forty-one thousand people worked there. Nine thousand work for the new system, and I hired, personally, I sat in the interviews, every one of them back. Nobody calls me the woman who did that.” The rest she put plainly: “We rebuilt the engine in flight because the alternative was the crash. Everybody agrees what taxes are for. Nobody had ever made the math match it. We did.” By its third year an internal review called it “better than Congress,” meaning only that it played no favorites. It appeared in two Senate hearings that year. Her team publishes one number each January, the gap between what the top and bottom deciles pay and what the code says they owe. In 2037 it was nine points. It is now two.

The Securities and Exchange Commission’s disclosure regime became something people finally used, because the systems that produced it could also read it and surface, in plain words, the risks a company had buried in three hundred pages of hedging. Ashland & Pike, the firm whose half-billion-dollar bet had opened the whole story, the firm that had reinvented itself as a technology company, folded. The firm’s last chairman declined, through a lawyer, to be interviewed.

The bar associations sued in 2033. The transparency these systems represented, disclosure that actually disclosed, a tax engine no lobbyist could bend, was what a great deal of money had spent a century learning to avoid. The bar fought to protect the franchise, dressing a guild’s self-interest in the noble language of professional responsibility. The surviving large firms, reborn as technology companies, lobbied to keep the new systems proprietary and closed rather than transparent and portable. For a few years they held.

When the financial system itself convulsed, the pushback ran out of ground to stand on. The same crisis that forced the I.R.S. and the S.E.C. to reinvent themselves stripped the moneyed interests of the one thing their resistance had always depended on, a system stable enough to be gamed. “They didn’t lose the argument,” Halloran said. “The ground went out from under it. Nobody out-argued anybody.”

By the end of the Correction, half the country’s law firms were gone, the commercial law of the United States ran on systems, and Halloran’s profession, the making of corporate peace out of paper and people, had become the orchestration of machines. “The Correction is when I understood the shape of it,” she said. “Money goes first when something is on fire. I kept waiting for the same thing to happen to the rest of the law. The courts. The real courts. It didn’t. The other window stayed open. I spent years trying to understand who was paying for it.”

2036 the correction

What the crisis did

A crisis of money accelerates the automation of money. When the question is survival and the medium is numbers, you reach for the thing that holds numbers perfectly.

How the resistance ended

The people who profited from the old opacity held for generations. What broke them was the dollar rather than the argument: opacity cannot be lobbied for in the middle of a collapse that opacity helped cause.

V.Elsewhere

The European Union wrote a right to data portability into law in 2018, as one clause in a privacy regulation, and nobody who voted for it was thinking about commercial arbitration.

The right said that a person, or a company, could take their data and move it, in a usable form, out of one system and into another. At the time it was treated, even in Europe, as a compliance headache, a box to tick, a reason to hire another officer with another certification.

When the dollar crisis tore through the world financial system, and it was the world’s system, not only America’s, every economy faced the same question: how fast could it rebuild commercial trust on something other than the old, failing plumbing? The answer rested on whether the records of who-owed-whom could be lifted cleanly and lawfully out of the institutions that were seizing up and fed into new ones. In the United States, that had to be improvised, firm by firm, clause by clause, in the middle of the emergency.

“The Americans think they led,” Anneke Roos, who spent twenty years in the European Commission’s data directorate, told me in a café near the Berlaymont. She had brought the regulation with her, printed, and did not open it. “They led on the spectacle. We led on the plumbing.” European commercial transactions moved into automated resolution with a speed that startled the Americans who had assumed they owned the future, precisely because the portability rules meant the data was already free to move. The patient, mocked-for-decades bureaucratic instinct to standardize and make things interoperable turned out to be the thing that let an entire continent pivot in eighteen months.

Halloran comes back to it. “Everybody wants to build the cathedral,” she said. “Nobody wants to lay the drains. But there is no cathedral without the drains. The whole transformation, ours and theirs, sat on top of a right most people couldn’t be bothered to read. The right to take your data and leave.”

Europe’s was one path. In the bloc that had decoupled from the Western system, the same machines arrived pointed the other way: automated, total, and opaque, a system that resolved your dispute in minutes and never once showed you why. A shipping agent I met in Istanbul had run the same invoice dispute through both systems, six weeks apart. The Western award ran to a page and a half. The other came back in nine seconds as a single line: RESOLVED, SELLER. He has kept the screenshot for two years. By 2040 there are several of these systems in the world, each with its own answer to what “fair” is allowed to mean and who gets to see the reasons, and a person who moves between two of them can find that the same facts produce two verdicts, and that no human anywhere is obliged to reconcile them.

2036 · 2038 elsewhere

The plumbing

Portability had been the law of the land in Europe for fifteen years while America improvised it clause by clause in the middle of the emergency. When the crisis came, the plumbing held.

Not one singularity

The same machines arrived elsewhere pointed the opposite way. By 2040 the map has fractured along the same lines the financial order did, and the open question is what happens at the borders, where the same facts produce two verdicts and nobody is obliged to reconcile them.

VI.The Second Window

Judge Naomi Frears hears human-harm cases, the criminal docket and the catastrophic personal-injury trials, in a mid-sized city I agreed not to name, in chambers she asked me not to describe, so I will say only that the chair I sat in was older than I am, Her courtroom is the second window, the one with the fee and the wait, and she has watched the commercial docket automate around it and stop at her door. There was a pilot. It ran for nineteen months in two counties in Oregon and produced sentences inside the guideline range ninety-four per cent of the time, against seventy-one for the judges. Frears has read the report. “It’s a good report,” she said. “They offered us the tools, you understand. Early on. There were very smart people who said the same systems that resolve a contract could resolve a sentencing, and they had the data to suggest the machine would be more consistent than I am. And they were probably right. I am not consistent. I have bad mornings.” She folded her hands. “And we said no. Not because the machine would be worse. Because we decided that some judgments require a human being to make them, and to be blamed for them, that a person facing the loss of their liberty, or a family facing the loss of a child, is owed the dignity of another person looking at them and taking responsibility for the decision. You can net out a debt. You can’t net out a dead kid. We decided not to try.”

Which window a matter belongs at is obvious in the easy cases, a breach of contract at the first, a homicide at the second, and contested in the middle. Employment was the first great battleground. When a person is fired, is that a money matter or a human matter? The corporations had an answer: the compliance systems that replaced human resources could detect a pattern of biased conduct, confirm it against the federal standard, and suspend the offender’s access without a meeting or a severance negotiation, a screen simply going dark. It was efficient, and by the numbers it was fairer. And it produced a steady stream of people who insisted that being cast out of your livelihood by a machine that would not look at you was precisely the kind of wound the second window was for. One of them, a regional sales manager I’ll call Dennis, described the morning his badge stopped working. “I thought it was the badge,” he said. “I went to the desk. The guard already knew. He was nicer about it than the email was, and the email was very nice.” The Wellness Office that rose in H.R.’s place was an attempt to split the difference: machines made the judgments, humans absorbed the people the judgments fell on.

“Everyone wants to know where the line is,” Frears said. “The second window is wherever we are still willing to pay for a human being, and every year someone makes the case that this category, too, belongs at the first. I don’t lie awake worrying that I’ll be replaced. I lie awake worrying that one day the case for replacing me will be correct, and that we’ll do it anyway, for the savings, and call it progress, and only realize twenty years later what we paved over.”

2040 the second window

Why the second window held

Frears was offered the tools and said no, on the ground that some judgments require a person to make them and to be blamed for them. The pilots suggested the machine would be the more consistent of the two. She grants the point.

What she lies awake about

Not being replaced. That the case for replacing her will one day be correct, and that it will be done for the savings and called progress.

VII.Which Line You Get In

Nobody is assigned to a window. You pick one, or a contract picks for you, and the fee at the second one goes up a little every year. The human courts, having lost the commercial docket, did not empty out. They filled with the people who found out too late which line they were in.

Judge Carol Lindqvist runs a hardship docket, and what comes before her is a debt, computed by a system that is never wrong about numbers. “That’s the whole trouble,” she told me. “In eleven years on this docket I have never once corrected an amount. What I correct is what the amount is about to do.” A man I’ll call Earl came before her with a medical debt that an automated system had calculated to the penny and an automated process had begun, lawfully and inexorably, to collect, against a retirement that would not survive the collecting. There was nothing to dispute about the money; the money was correct. Earl is seventy-three and had brought a manila envelope that nobody asked to see. “I’m not saying I don’t owe it,” he told me in the hallway, before he was called. “I’m saying I’m seventy-three. I don’t know how you put that in the computer.” What Lindqvist was there to decide was the thing no system could: whether a correct debt ought to be allowed to end a man. The eviction from the second room, in October, had been hers too; the family kept the apartment through the winter. “Collections is math,” she said. “What comes to my courtroom is everything the math can’t finish. I’m the human you get to appeal to when the math is right and the result is monstrous.”

She let Earl’s collection run at eleven dollars a month against a debt of sixty-one thousand, which at that rate will never be paid. The envelope, when he opened it in the hallway afterward, held his wife’s death certificate and a photograph of a boat.

Halloran was there that morning. I had asked her to come and she had said no, and then she was in the back row in a coat, and she stayed through Earl. In the hallway afterward she said the collection system had been one of hers, an early one, licensed from her company to the hospital chain, and that it had worked exactly as designed. Then she said she had to make a call.

The other half of her docket is newer, and stranger. A contractor I’ll call Pia watched an automated commercial dispute, decided against her in four minutes over a data error she had never known existed, cascade through nine self-executing agreements in ninety seconds, until contracts she had honored for years declared themselves in default, and there was no second window in any of them. The decision against her, which she showed me on her phone, was four sentences long and correct in every one of them. “I kept asking who decided,” she said. “And the answer was that nobody decided. There was no one to be angry at. There was no one to forgive me.” The only door left open to her was the oldest one: a human court, a human judge, a person with the standing to look at a flawless outcome and call it wrong. “You can’t apologize to a model,” Lindqvist said. “You can’t ask it for mercy. Somebody has to be able to do those things, or what you have is a collections agency with a flag out front.”

I told Rosa Delgado about Pia, later, on the phone. VIII.The Orchestrators

Ninety-nine per cent of large companies, Law.com reported, have eliminated the chief legal officer’s role. At Halloran’s old company the title in its place is Chief Intelligence Officer, and it belongs to a thirty-four-year-old named Devin Cho, who inherited her authority, distributed now across every commercial system in the building. We met in what used to be the general counsel’s conference room, which no longer has a table in it; her office is a wellness room. He is slight, in a navy suit with the store crease still in the sleeve. “I don’t fill her shoes,” Cho said. “There are no shoes. There’s a layer that runs the company’s whole commercial life, and I keep my hands on it.” He has never met Halloran. He learned her name from an org chart. “It pays better than the law was ever going to,” he said. Bell’s title now is rule-of-law strategist; the job is deciding where the human hand rests on the machine. “I tell the young ones the machines took the filing and left us the fights,” Bell said. “Some of them even believe me.”

Twice while we spoke, something chimed at Cho’s wrist, and twice he silenced it without looking. The third time he looked, read it, and said a supplier in Manila had repriced itself and that it was fine. He answered it by typing four characters. When I asked what they meant he said, “It means I saw it,” and that this was logged, and that this was most of the job. I asked him the question I’d been carrying since Frears’s chambers: what kept his role human, when the chief legal officer’s had dissolved? He was quiet a long time. “On the commercial side? Nothing structural,” he said. “The deals could orchestrate themselves tomorrow. Mostly. What keeps a human here is, it’s the same thing that keeps Judge Frears on her bench. Somebody has to be accountable. Nobody is going to subpoena the stack. They’ll subpoena me. The day that stops mattering to people, I’m gone too. I try not to think about the words ‘for now.’”

IX.The Holdouts

Pondera County District Court, in Conrad, Montana, sits every third Tuesday.

It is not the last human courtroom in America, the criminal and injury courts are human in every state, and it is not even the last to hear business the old way. The National Center for State Courts counts forty-one of them: rural circuits, stubborn jurisdictions, a handful of specialty dockets that, by local statute or orneriness, still keep a second window open for commercial disputes, free, if the parties want it. Lawyers call them the holdouts. Reyes, in Maryland, calls them a hedge, a capacity to do it the human way, kept on the shelf in case the country ever wants it back.

Conrad is the most famous of them, because it leaned into the role. Montana wrote its holdout status into statute and onto its tourism site, somewhere between Glacier and the Testicle Festival, and people fly in from across the country to watch a contract dispute heard by a person.

Judge Eleanor Brandt presides, climbing two flights of stairs to do it. It was the last interview of the year, and I had run out of questions that were not the same question. She is seventy-one, with reading glasses on a beaded chain, and the day I visited she heard a dispute between a rancher and a contractor over a barn, exactly the sort of who-owes-whom that Rosa Delgado settled in four minutes on her phone, and that Antoine Reyes built a machine in Maryland to resolve at scale. It took Brandt most of a day. The men testified and contradicted themselves; one of them wept about something that had nothing to do with the barn. At the end she ruled, mostly for the contractor, with one concession to the rancher: the barn’s cupola, which his father had built, which was worth about nine hundred dollars, and which neither man had mentioned. She had noticed it in a photograph the contractor entered to prove something else.

“I know what I am,” Brandt told me afterward, rewrapping half a sandwich. “I’m a reenactor. The machines do the money better than I do, faster, cheaper, fairer on average, and I’ve made my peace with that on the good days. I keep this window open for one reason. To remind people that we chose. Somebody decided which line you stand in, on purpose, and a choice made on purpose is a choice you can make again. People are starting to forget it was a choice. I’m the reminder. That’s worth a Tuesday, I think.”

Afterword

I have been sharing a scenario about the future of law. A fully automated commercial legal system by 2040, with human courts kept for the matters that turn on justice and humanity. The most common response is that it is unrealistic.

It is. As a prediction, it is almost certainly wrong.

A scenario works as a stress test. You build the vivid version of a future so you can argue with it, and the argument shows you which assumptions are load bearing. So here is the working version. For the world in that story to arrive, every one of these has to come true. Treat each one as a signal to watch.

CONDITION 1

Data has to move

Portable, interoperable, structured data is the foundation. Without it, nothing else on this list matters. Europe wrote portability into law years ago. The US still has not.

CONDITION 2

Models have to get good at structure

Summarizing a record is solved. Turning messy public records into representations that survive real use, clean family trees, defensible timelines, source-linked exports, is the unsolved part.

CONDITION 3

Outputs have to be verifiable

Nobody defers to a system they cannot audit. Adoption depends on proof: the ability to show why a decision was reached and to prove the record behind it was not altered. The scenario assumes this, and I think it is the hardest piece. Capability is the easy half. Trust is the binding constraint.

CONDITION 4

Institutions have to say yes

Agencies, courts, bar rules, ethics opinions. This is slow and political by design, and it should be.

CONDITION 5

Accountability has to be settled

When the system is wrong, a human has to answer for it. You cannot put a model in front of a regulator. Until that is resolved, the second window stays open, which is exactly why the scenario keeps it.

CONDITION 6

The economics have to flip

Outcome-based value over the billable hour, against incumbents who profit from today’s friction.

CONDITION 7

The workforce has to move with it

New roles, real reskilling, and law schools that teach people to build.

CONDITION 8

It probably takes a shock

The compressed timeline runs on a crisis. Absent one, this is a diffusion measured in decades, not in fifteen years.

Read that list and the skeptics look right. None of it is close to finished. Read it as a checklist and several items have already started to move, which is the point of the exercise: the order of the dominoes, and which ones are already tipping.

For product and legal teams, the takeaway is the same either way: build for the work to be trusted, proven, and accounted for, and you are building for the realistic version of this future, whenever it arrives.
